ATM behavior in a post-cash economy
Wells Fargo
— 01 / case file
Wells Fargo · 2025 · Lead Researcher

Tested whether the ATM affordance vocabulary still made sense to people who had stopped using cash for daily purchases. Heavy cash users planned by bill count; light cash users planned by total amount. The existing UI privileged the bill-count model.

The redesign exposed both anchors simultaneously and recovered task success on denomination customization.

Sometimes the outcome shows no meaningful difference between designs — that in itself is meaningful.

Notable null result: no significant difference between dark and light prototype conditions (46% vs 50%, p = .31). Reported explicitly rather than mining for a secondary win.

A redesign of the premier-tier mobile experience underperformed on the metric the business cared about most: time-to-advisor. The design team believed the metric was lagging. The business team believed the design was failing. Research had to settle that disagreement with evidence.

Reaction-time data confirmed the visual hierarchy was steering attention away from the action. Customers spent 14 seconds longer on the home screen before tapping anything.

To increase value and attract more high net-worth customers, designers came up with an app experience that they felt was more premium, but does that come at a cost of usability?

2022
End-to-end account-opening journey
Wells Fargo
— 03 / case file
Wells Fargo · 2022 · Lead Researcher

Two product teams owned discrete phases of an account opening journey: discovery to sign-up, and verification to funding. Customers experienced the whole thing as disjointed, lacking a proper welcome as a new customer.

Compiled a journey from five moderated usability tests. It was quickly visible that the apply experience was disjointed from the onboarding experience, lacking a robust confirmation or celebration of task completion, relying on just a badly formatted email to bridge the experiences together. The solution I came up with was to facilitate a workshop between the two teams to address the gaps.

Discrete teams working on separate phases caused a disjointed experience for new customers — the solution was to create a workshop to mitigate the gap.

2020
Decision dashboards under crisis load
Kaiser Permanente
— 04 / case file
Kaiser Permanente · 2020 · UX Designer & Researcher

In the first months of COVID, hospital supervisors and regional executives needed to make decisions hourly with incomplete data. Existing dashboards were built for steady-state operations and broke down under crisis tempo.

Embedded with operations leadership for 14 months. Two-week iteration cycles against actual decisions being made that week.

In a crisis, leadership doesn't read dashboards. They scan them. The job is to design for the scan, not the read.

Executives needed three numbers at a glance, in stable positions, with one clear signal for "is this normal." Supervisors needed the same data with a different temporal frame.

A B2B SaaS product had strong SEO landing performance but a steep drop at trial signup. The funnel team had hypotheses; nobody had talked to the users who dropped.

IBM · 2019 · User Researcher

Watson OpenScale was building KPI and drift monitoring tools for model risk managers in financial services. The concepts had been informed by secondary research and SME conversations — but hadn't been validated with the model owners and validators who would actually live inside the workflow.

Four moderated remote concept walkthroughs with model risk managers, model owners, and validators across insurance, technology, and financial services. — GEOG 820 · SFSU · Spring 2026
Park and Ride or Placemaking?
A mobility profile of Honolulu
— the argument

Honolulu's constrained geography forces a level of land use that requires drastic change. Crammed between ocean and two mountain ranges, the city has run out of space to keep solving traffic with more roads. It is the fourth-densest urban area in the U.S. and the most congested medium-sized city in the country. The H-1 Freeway operates at "F" level of service. More residents have moved off-island than on for 23 of the last 25 years.

Honolulu's story could serve as a case study for how a car-dependent American city might reduce car and fossil fuel use. Mobility is connected to whether residents can afford to stay — especially Native Hawaiians and long-rooted residents with profound ties to the island.

The paper traces how the island's site (a linear coastal plain hemmed in by volcanoes) and situation (Polynesian ahupuaʻa → plantation capitalism → military annexation → tourism) produced today's car-dependent urban form, and then evaluates how the 2010 Environmental Impact Statement for the Skyline rail project — and Skyline as built — attempts to course-correct.

— fig 1 / skyline alignment and density
ArcGIS map showing the Skyline alignment, Urban Honolulu boundary, Primary Urban Center, rail stations, census tract centroids, and 3- and 5-mile station buffers. The densest centroid cluster remains concentrated near the Honolulu core, while the operating line currently reaches ʻEwa/Kapolei and Middle Street/Airport.
— fig 2 / from first proposal to first ride: 57 years
Skyline took 57 years from Mayor Blaisdell's first proposal in 1966 to revenue service in 2023. The Highway Users Alliance — car dealers, tour bus operators, taxi companies, and asphalt contractors — opposed every iteration.
— fig 3 / public transit commuters vs. solo drivers
Public transit commuters in Urban Honolulu are more likely than solo drivers to be renters, foreign-born, below poverty, without vehicle access, and facing 60+ minute commutes. Values are reproduced from the supplied chart/table.
— what the EIS missed

The 2010 Final EIS succeeded in outlining the technical and economic case for rail. It correctly identified that the H-1 corridor cannot be widened and that ʻEwa and Kapolei would absorb half the island's growth. It selected steel-wheel-on-steel-rail over mag-lev on cost and reliability grounds.

But it left the rider behind in three specific ways:

→ No station-level community integration. Other than zoning recommendations, the EIS lacks guidance for connecting stations to parks, schools, libraries, or commerce.
→ Last-mile detail absent. Cycling integration is mentioned in principle. No detailed network is proposed.
→ No human-scale renderings. Guideway visual impact is modeled; station design at rider scale is not.

A February 2025 city audit confirmed the consequences. Skyline runs with 99.2% operational reliability — and peaks at 3,637 daily rides against an 8,000 benchmark. Cost-per-rider doubled from $47 to $83 in six months. Auditors timed bus-rail trips at 87 minutes versus 68 by car. Wayfinding is English-only; ADA groups have not been consulted since 2020.

A system can run reliably and still fail to provide resilient urban infrastructure. The trains arrive on time, but the destinations, payment systems, sidewalks, and language access that make transit usable showed a disconnection from the other stakeholders.

Segment 2's October 2025 opening — extending the line to the airport — produced an immediate 2.4× step-change in ridership. The State is now negotiating an MOU with Tokyu Group, the Tokyo conglomerate that built Shibuya, to import Japanese TOD expertise. The recognition that the original planning model did not deliver is itself the finding.

Whether rail functions as redistribution or as another investment that prices residents out depends on the question Oʻahu has been answering, badly, for over a century: who are these new neighborhoods for?
